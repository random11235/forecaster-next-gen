Which forecasts go wrong

Actual finish outside the forecast's 10th–90th percentile range

A perfect forecast misses this range about 12% of the time here (dashed line), not 20%, because finish dates are whole weeks and many actual finishes land exactly on the P10 or P90 week. Even Steady misses more than that: ten weeks of history make the range too narrow. Trends, team changes and spells of good or bad weeks miss far more often. Lumpy history misses a little more; one odd week barely matters.

Split-halves error is the standard error, plus noise

Split-halves error against relative standard error

Each dot is one history of 4 to 20 weeks. The line is the best fit through zero. The two measure the same thing; the relative standard error is exact, needs no random splits and changes with the data alone.

What each signal flags

Share of histories flagged, by team type

Each cell is a share of that team type's simulated histories. The red top row is the problem to catch: the share whose actual finish fell outside the forecast's 80% range. A perfect forecast lands there about 12% of the time (whole-week finish dates), so near 12% is healthy and higher is worse. The grey rows below are neither good nor bad on their own; darker just means the signal fires more often. A useful signal is dark under the red cells and pale elsewhere. Split-halves and standard error are darkest under lumpy and one-odd-week teams, whose forecasts are fine, and fire on every outlier history.

Can the tool tell an EM which pattern their team has?

Instead of an alarm, name the pattern and suggest what to look at. A simple rule set, built from the signals above, was tried on fresh histories of each team type. Read across a row: the shaded diagonal is how often that team type is named correctly. One odd week, Lumpy and Steady are recognised reliably even from short history. Speeding up and Team change are often confused with each other, so they are better named together as "pace changing", and that needs about twice the history. Spells are rarely recognised, and short histories of every changing team are often called Steady.

What this suggests

  • Replace split-halves with the relative standard error. Same signal, no randomness, and the 25% / 75% bands become about 16% / 47%. Keep it as a "thin data" note, not a warning that the forecast is wrong.
  • Add a trend test. Mann–Kendall fires on — of steady histories and — of speeding-up ones. Combined with lag-1 autocorrelation, flagged forecasts miss — of the time against — when clear.
  • Show leave-one-out influence as an outlier note. It singles out the one odd week every time and almost never fires otherwise, so it can name the week to check.
  • Ten weeks can't prove a trend. Even the best test misses about half of the speeding-up histories. A trend flag should prompt a look at recent weeks or a shorter window, not block the forecast.
